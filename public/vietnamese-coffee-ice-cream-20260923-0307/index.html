The Phin-to-Freeze Method: How to Make Vietnamese Coffee Ice Cream That Actually Tastes Like Vietnam

By Tien Nguyen

Most Vietnamese coffee ice cream is a lie.

Not a malicious one — more of a shortcut. Somebody stirs instant coffee into a custard base, adds a ribbon of condensed milk, freezes it, and calls it a day. The result is sweet, brown, and vaguely coffee-ish. It tastes like coffee ice cream that once flew over Vietnam at 30,000 feet.

Real Vietnamese coffee ice cream tastes like cà phê sữa đá — the bitter punch of dark-roasted robusta, the velvet of condensed milk, the slow sweet finish that lingers after the glass is empty. And you can absolutely make it at home.

Here's the thing: the craft isn't in the ice cream. It's in the coffee. Learn to brew a proper concentrated base with a phin filter, and the rest is mostly waiting.

This is the Phin-to-Freeze Method. Let's get into it.

Why Most Vietnamese Coffee Ice Cream Falls Flat

Almost every recipe you'll find online skips the one step that matters most: brewing a genuinely concentrated, properly extracted Vietnamese coffee base.

Instead, you get one of two things. Either a generic coffee ice cream with a Vietnamese name slapped on it, or a simplified recipe that says "brew strong coffee and add condensed milk." That second one is closer, but still incomplete — because "strong" means something specific when you're working with robusta, a phin, and a freezer.

Here's what's missing from the average coffee ice cream recipe:

  • Ratio discipline. Vietnamese phin coffee is brewed at roughly 1:4 to 1:5 coffee-to-water by weight — far more concentrated than a drip machine.
  • Bean choice. Robusta, not arabica, defines the flavor.
  • The bloom. A short pre-infusion changes extraction dramatically.
  • Cold-base math. Ice cream dilutes flavor as it freezes. Your base needs to be more intense than what you'd drink.

Fix those four things and your ice cream goes from "nice" to "wait, you made this?"

The Science of Vietnamese Coffee, Briefly

Coffee arrived in Vietnam with French colonists in the mid-19th century, and within decades the country had reshaped global coffee production entirely. As >Atlas Obscura's history of Vietnamese coffee explains, Vietnam is now the world's second-largest coffee producer and the largest producer of robusta by a wide margin.

That matters for your dessert, because robusta is a different animal from the arabica you're probably used to.

Compared to arabica, robusta beans carry roughly double the caffeine, far less acidity, and a heavier, more syrupy body. Their flavor leans earthy, chocolatey, and nutty, with a bitterness that reads as bold rather than sour. That bitter edge is exactly why condensed milk exists in the equation — it doesn't just sweeten, it balances.

Worth noting: the intense, slightly smoky character of Vietnamese coffee also comes from how the beans are typically roasted. Many traditional roasters use butter and sometimes a touch of sugar or cocoa during the roast, which deepens the color and adds a caramelized richness. That's a flavor you cannot fake with a supermarket can of pre-ground arabica.

Step 1: Brew the Base With a Phin

The phin (pronounced "fin") is a small stainless steel drip filter that sits directly on your cup. It's four pieces: the cup or glass, the filter chamber, the press (a small disc with a stem), and the lid.

It looks fussy. It isn't. It's just unfamiliar to most Western kitchens.

What you need for the base

  • 25–30 g Vietnamese coffee, medium-coarse grind (like coarse sea salt)
  • 150 ml hot water, just off the boil — around 195–205°F / 90–96°C
  • 1 phin filter
  • 1 vessel (a glass measuring cup works great)

The walkthrough

  1. Preheat the phin. Pour a splash of hot water through the empty filter into your cup, swirl, and discard. This warms the metal so your coffee doesn't hit a cold surface.
  2. Add the coffee. Spoon the grounds into the chamber and level them gently. Do not tamp. This isn't espresso.
  3. Bloom. Pour just enough hot water to cover the grounds — maybe 30 ml — and wait 30 seconds. You'll see the grounds swell and darken. This releases trapped CO2 and sets up an even extraction.
  4. Insert the press. Set the press disc on top. It should rest on the grounds with light resistance. If it slides down freely, you used too little coffee. If you have to push hard, you used too much.
  5. Fill and cover. Pour the remaining hot water to the top of the chamber and put the lid on. Now leave it alone.
  6. Wait. A good phin drip takes 4 to 6 minutes. If your coffee is racing through in 90 seconds, your grind is too coarse. If nothing comes out after 8 minutes, it's too fine.

What you end up with is a small, intense, almost syrupy brew — the same stuff that becomes cà phê sữa đá when poured over ice and condensed milk.

For the ice cream base, make two batches of this and let them cool completely. You want roughly 200 ml of concentrated coffee total. Do not dilute it. Your phin filter brewing guide habits from drinking coffee don't apply here — you're brewing an ingredient, not a beverage.

No phin? Here's the workaround

You can approximate the result with a pour-over cone and a fine mesh, or a moka pot for a denser brew. A French press works in a pinch but leaves sediment that will settle at the bottom of your custard. Whatever you use, aim for the same concentration and accept that the texture of the ritual — the slow drip, the smell filling the kitchen — is part of the pleasure you're skipping.

Step 2: Choose Your Beans Like You Mean It

If you take one thing from this article, take this: the beans decide the flavor. Everything else is finesse.

Here's what to look for when buying Vietnamese coffee beans:

  • Robusta or a robusta-forward blend. This is the backbone of Vietnamese coffee flavor. A 100% arabica "Vietnamese style" coffee is a contradiction in terms.
  • Dark or medium-dark roast. You want the roast character to survive freezing. Light roasts go flat and sour in ice cream.
  • Freshness. Whole bean, roasted within the last month if possible. Pre-ground coffee oxidizes fast, and that staleness gets amplified in a frozen base.
  • Butter-roasted or traditional. If the label mentions butter roasting or a traditional Vietnamese roast profile, that's a good sign.

Popular options in the U.S. include Trung Nguyên, Nguyen Coffee Supply, and Chestbrew. One caution: Café du Monde, which shows up in a lot of "Vietnamese coffee" searches, is actually a New Orleans chicory blend. It's delicious, but it's not the same drink.

Step 3: A Note on Roast and Grind Size — and Your Freezer

Most people don't think about grind size when they're making dessert. They should.

Grind size affects extraction, which affects flavor intensity. A coarser grind gives you a cleaner, lighter brew. A finer grind gives you more body and more bitterness. For ice cream, lean slightly coarser than you would for drinking — excess sediment in the base creates a gritty texture once frozen.

Roast level affects how the flavor survives the freeze. Cold mutes aromatics. That's why ice cream always needs to be seasoned more aggressively than the same dish served warm. Darker roasts hold up better because their flavor compounds are more stable and more concentrated.

There's a reason behind this: cold temperatures numb your taste receptors. A base that tastes perfect at room temperature will taste weak at 10°F. Plan for it by over-extracting slightly and tasting your base cold before churning.

Step 4: The Condensed Milk Conversation

Now we get to the other half of the equation.

Condensed milk in Vietnamese coffee isn't a modernization or a shortcut — it's a legacy of scarcity. When fresh dairy was hard to come by in mid-century Vietnam, the sweetened, shelf-stable canned version became the default. It stuck because it's genuinely excellent: thick, caramel-adjacent, and rich enough to stand up to robusta's bitterness.

For ice cream, condensed milk does three jobs at once:

  1. Sweetens without the watery dilution of simple syrup
  2. Adds fat and body, contributing to a creamier final texture
  3. Prevents large ice crystals, because sugar interferes with freezing

That third point is why condensed milk desserts — from flan to no-churn ice cream — work so reliably. It's chemistry doing you a favor. You can read more about the fascinating history and global spread of Vietnamese coffee culture over at >Eater's Vietnamese coffee coverage.

Step 5: The Ratio (Taste-Tested)

Here's where the rubber meets the road. After a lot of testing, this is the ratio I keep coming back to for a home-churned batch that serves 6 to 8.

The Phin-to-Freeze Base

Ingredient Amount Notes
Concentrated phin coffee 200 ml Cooled completely
Sweetened condensed milk 1 can (14 oz / 397 g) Full-fat, standard
Heavy cream 1½ cups At least 36% fat
Whole milk ½ cup Adds body without heaviness
Fine sea salt ¼ tsp Non-negotiable
Optional: vodka or rum 1 tbsp For softer scoop texture

Why these numbers:

  • The 200 ml of coffee is roughly 1 part coffee concentrate to 2.5 parts dairy by volume. Strong enough to taste clearly, not so strong it turns bitter.
  • One full can of condensed milk sounds like a lot. It is. It's also the traditional ratio — the equivalent of a cà phê sữa đá where the milk is generous and the coffee punches through.
  • The salt is the quiet hero. It doesn't make the ice cream salty; it makes the coffee and caramel notes louder.
  • The alcohol (completely optional) lowers the freezing point just enough to keep the texture scoopable straight from the freezer.

If you want a less sweet result, cut the condensed milk to ¾ can and add 2 tablespoons of extra heavy cream. If you want it sweeter and more traditional, keep the full can. Taste the base before churning — a chilled spoonful, not a warm one.

Step 6: Churn It, or Don't

If you have an ice cream maker

  1. Whisk all ingredients until fully combined and smooth.
  2. Chill the base for at least 4 hours — overnight is better. A cold base churns faster and produces smaller crystals.
  3. Churn according to your machine's instructions, usually 20–25 minutes, until it looks like soft-serve.
  4. Transfer to a loaf pan, press plastic wrap against the surface, and freeze 4 hours minimum.

If you want a benchmark for consistency, >Serious Eats' Vietnamese coffee ice cream recipe is a solid reference point for a custard-style base, and >David Lebovitz's version is a great read for anyone curious about how a professional pastry mindset approaches the same idea.

Coffee ice cream without an ice cream maker

This is the part where most people give up. Don't.

The no-churn method relies entirely on whipped cream and condensed milk, and it works remarkably well here because those are two of our core ingredients anyway.

  1. Whip 2 cups cold heavy cream to stiff peaks.
  2. In a separate bowl, whisk 1 can condensed milk + 200 ml cooled coffee + ¼ tsp salt.
  3. Fold the whipped cream into the coffee mixture in three additions. Be gentle — you're preserving air.
  4. Pour into a loaf pan, cover, and freeze 6 hours or overnight.

The result is slightly less dense than churned ice cream but unmistakably smooth and intensely flavored. For a Vietnamese coffee dessert that takes fifteen minutes of active work, it's hard to beat.

Phin-to-Freeze Vietnamese Coffee Ice Cream

⏱ Prep: 20 min + chill overnight; Churn: 25 min; Freeze: 4 hours · ☕ About 1 quart

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup dark-roasted robusta coffee, coarsely ground
  • 1 cup boiling water
  • 1 can (14 oz) sweetened condensed milk
  • 1 cup heavy cream
  • 1 cup whole milk
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon vodka (optional, for texture)

Steps

  1. Set up phin filter. Add coffee, shake to level, place filter press on top.
  2. Bloom: Pour 2 tablespoons hot water, wait 30 seconds.
  3. Brew: Fill phin with remaining hot water, cover, let drip completely (about 4-5 minutes). You should have about 3/4 cup concentrated coffee.
  4. In a bowl, whisk condensed milk with coffee until smooth.
  5. Add heavy cream, milk, salt, and vodka if using. Whisk to combine.
  6. Chill mixture thoroughly in refrigerator (at least 4 hours, preferably overnight).
  7. Churn in ice cream maker according to manufacturer's instructions.
  8. Transfer to container, freeze for at least 4 hours to firm up.
  9. Scoop and serve, perhaps with extra condensed milk drizzle.

The Golden Rule The craft isn't in the ice cream—it's in the coffee. Nail a concentrated phin brew, and the rest is just waiting.

Can I use arabica instead of robusta?

Robusta is traditional and provides the bold, bitter backbone that defines Vietnamese coffee. Arabica will taste milder and less authentic.

Do I really need a phin filter?

Yes, a phin is essential for the proper extraction and concentration. A drip machine or French press won't give you the same intensity.

Why is my ice cream icy or hard?

The base must be extra concentrated because freezing dulls flavors. Also, a tablespoon of vodka or bourbon can help keep it scoopable.

Can I make it without an ice cream maker?

Yes, you can use the no-churn method: whip 2 cups heavy cream to stiff peaks, fold in the coffee-condensed milk mixture, and freeze.
